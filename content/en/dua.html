In brief

  • The Prophet ﷺ taught one supplication for this night when his wife ʿĀʾishah asked what she should say if she knew it had come.
  • In transliteration it reads Allāhumma innaka ʿafuwwun tuḥibbu l-ʿafwa fa-ʿfu ʿannī: “O Allah, You are Pardoning and You love to pardon, so pardon me.”
  • Two collections preserve it with authentic chains; one adds a single word of praise.
  • It may be repeated at any point of the night, alongside personal supplication in any language.

Of all the words a believer might say on Laylat al-Qadr (لَيْلَةُ الْقَدْر, “the Night of Decree”), one supplication stands apart, because the Prophet ﷺ chose it himself in answer to a direct question. ʿĀʾishah, his wife, asked what she should say if she came to know which night was the Night of Decree. His reply was a single sentence, and its brevity is part of its strength: it praises Allah by one attribute and asks Him for one thing.1

This page sets out the Arabic text, its transliteration and meaning, the sources that preserve it, the difference between their wordings, and guidance on how to use the supplication through the last ten nights of Ramadan.

The duʿāʾ the Prophet ﷺ taught

Where the supplication comes from

Two of the six canonical hadith collections record the exchange between ʿĀʾishah and the Prophet ﷺ. Ibn Mājah preserves the wording shown above.2 Al-Tirmidhī records the same exchange and judges the hadith ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ, a grade that places it among sound narrations; the edition on which this guide relies grades it ṣaḥīḥ.3

What the words mean

The supplication rests on the root ʿ-f-w, which carries the sense of effacing a mark until nothing of it remains. ʿAfuww, the divine name used here, describes one who pardons in that thorough way and does so repeatedly. The request that follows, fa-ʿfu ʿannī, asks for exactly that: the removal of the fault itself, together with its consequence.

The order of the sentence matters. The believer first acknowledges who Allah is, then states what He loves, and only then asks. Each clause prepares the next, so that the request arrives as a natural conclusion drawn from the praise. For the holiest night of the year, the Prophet ﷺ taught a sentence a child can learn in a minute, and its compactness allows it to be repeated through a whole night without strain.

It is striking that the supplication asks for pardon, when the night invites every kind of request. The choice directs the believer to the need that underlies all others. A person who is pardoned stands clear with his Lord, and the Prophet ﷺ promised that whoever stands in prayer on this night out of faith and in hope of reward will have his previous sins forgiven.4

When and how to say it

The hadith sets no count and no fixed moment. ʿĀʾishah asked what to say on the night itself, and the answer applies to the whole of it, from sunset until dawn. Because the exact night is unknown, the sound course is to use the supplication on every night of the last ten, with particular attention to the odd nights.5

Say the words with understanding. A believer who knows what ʿafuwwun means, and who has a particular fault in mind when asking for pardon, prays with a present heart. Repetition serves that purpose when it deepens attention; it defeats it when it becomes a count to finish.

Supplicating in your own words

Nothing in the sources restricts the night to set formulas. Alongside the duʿāʾ taught to ʿĀʾishah, a believer may ask in any language for anything lawful, for himself and for others. Many find it useful to prepare a short written list before the last ten nights begin, so that personal needs and the names of people who asked for prayers are not forgotten in the hours of the night.

Common questions

Must the duʿāʾ be said in Arabic?

The Prophetic wording is best learned in Arabic, and it is short enough for anyone to memorise. Personal supplication may be made in any language; Allah hears every tongue.

Is there a special prayer attached to it?

The authentic reports attach no particular prayer, number of units or recitation to this supplication. Lists that prescribe fixed prayers for individual nights have no support in the narrations cited on this page.

May a woman who is not praying because of menstruation say it?

Yes. The supplication is a spoken remembrance and needs no ritual purity. ʿĀʾishah, to whom it was taught, asked about the night as a whole, and supplication remains open to every believer throughout it.

What if I do not know which night it is?

That uncertainty is intended. The Prophet ﷺ was once about to announce the night, and the knowledge was withdrawn; he told his Companions that this might be better for them.6 Saying the supplication on every night of the last ten ensures that the one night is not missed.
