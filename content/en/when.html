No question about Laylat al-Qadr is asked more often than when it falls. The sources give a precise answer and a deliberately incomplete one. They narrow the night to a span of ten, then to the odd nights within it, and they record that the Prophet ﷺ knew the exact night on one occasion and that the knowledge was withdrawn for the benefit of his community.1

What the narrations say about particular nights

Beyond the general instruction, several authentic reports name specific nights. They are best read together, because each describes a particular year or a particular sign.

Scholars reconcile these reports in two main ways. Some hold that the night moves among the odd nights from year to year, so that each report describes the year in which it was seen. Others hold that it is fixed, most often on the 27th, citing the oath of Ubayy ibn Kaʿb and the narration of Muʿāwiyah.56 Both views agree on the practice that follows from them: give every one of the last ten nights its due, with the odd nights foremost.

Why the exact night is hidden

ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit reported that the Prophet ﷺ came out to tell his Companions the date, found two men quarrelling, and returned to say that the knowledge had been taken away, adding that this might be better for them.7 The report teaches two lessons at once. Quarrelling carries a cost that can reach a whole community, and the concealment itself has a purpose: a believer who does not know the night seeks it on ten nights, and ten nights of worship are more than one.

How the nights are counted

In the Islamic calendar a day begins at sunset. The night of the 27th therefore begins on the evening that closes the 26th day of fasting and ends at dawn on the 27th day. Readers who plan by the civil calendar should look to the evening before the date they expect.

Different countries, different nights

Because Ramadan may begin on different days in different countries, the odd nights do not fall on the same calendar evening everywhere. A believer follows the Ramadan his own community observes and counts the nights from its first day. Differences of time zone do not change this: the night runs from sunset to dawn wherever one is.

Practice also differs between the main branches of Islam. Sunni Muslims most commonly observe the 27th night, while Shiʿi Muslims give particular weight to the 23rd.9 Both traditions treat the last ten nights as the season of search.

Is tonight Laylat al-Qadr?

No one can say with certainty on the night itself. The signs recorded in the hadith, such as the sun rising without rays, are mostly seen the following morning, so they confirm a night once it has passed. The sound course is to treat every night of the last ten as the one, and to leave the certainty to Allah. The page on the signs of Laylat al-Qadr sets out each sign with its source.

Common questions

Does Laylat al-Qadr change every year?

The reports that name different nights in different years lead many scholars to hold that it moves among the odd nights. Others hold it fixed on the 27th. Either way, the practice is the same.

What if Ramadan has only twenty-nine days?

The 29th night still occurs, because it precedes the 29th day. Only the 30th night is lost in a twenty-nine-day month.

Could the night fall outside Ramadan?

ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd once remarked that whoever prays at night throughout the year will come upon it. Ubayy ibn Kaʿb answered with an oath that the night is in Ramadan, and named the 27th.10 The Qurʾān settles the month: the Qurʾān was sent down in Ramadan, and it was sent down on the Night of Decree, so the night lies within Ramadan.

Should I stay awake only on the odd nights?

The odd nights carry the strongest indication, and a believer with limited strength may reasonably concentrate on them. The Prophet ﷺ himself gave all ten nights his effort, and whoever can follow that example gains the night wherever it falls.

Preparing before the tenth night ends

The last ten nights begin at sunset on the twentieth day of fasting, and they are easy to reach unprepared. A few decisions made in the first two thirds of Ramadan make the difference: arranging work and family commitments so that late nights are possible, learning the supplication taught for the night, choosing the chapters to recite in voluntary prayer, and setting aside what one intends to give in charity.
